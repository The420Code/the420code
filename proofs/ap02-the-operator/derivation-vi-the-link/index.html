Derivation VI

The Link

Mismatched drift makes a link a permanent tax on the more efficient agent; zero coupling is the safe default until match is verified. Across a finite operator network, the persistent set is confined to the net-positive class; net-negative, parasitic, and defecting configurations reach the no-return surface. Quantitative within-class matching criterion owed (Debt D1).

Why isolation is safe and mismatch is fatal

You are stable alone. You can maintain yourself. But alone, you are bounded — your scale cannot exceed your own capacity. Growth requires coupling. And coupling introduces the possibility of contagious failure.

This derivation proves the most uncomfortable truth about relationships: coupling is safe only when both sides can pay their own drift.

When they cannot, the link becomes a drain — and the mathematics is merciless about the cost.

Statement of the Claim

Claim: Isolation maximises stability but bounds scale. Coupling permits growth but introduces contagious instability. Coupling is safe only when both sides can hold imported energy as structure.

When drift and capacity are mismatched, the link becomes a gradient-driven leak into the higher-loss system.

The Coupled System

Introduce two agents with stored structured energy E₁, E₂ and coupling strength k:

Ė₁ = −a₁E₁ + u₁ − k(E₁ − E₂)

Ė₂ = −a₂E₂ + u₂ + k(E₁ − E₂)

Coupling moves energy down gradients. It does not create energy.

Parasitic Coupling (The Sink)

Assume E₁ ≫ E₂. Energy flows from agent 1 to agent 2. If agent 2 has higher loss than it can service (a₂ large and/or u₂ insufficient), incoming energy is dissipated immediately. No structure forms.

The gradient persists.

Result: coupling raises the required input for the low-drift agent without producing stable increase in the high-drift agent. The link converts control authority into heat. This is negative-sum coupling: sink dynamics.

Matched Coupling

If both agents can pay their own drift and their operating points are comparable, the gradient remains small. A practical low-leak matching criterion is approximate parity in drift rates and control margins:

a₁ ≈ a₂ and u₁ ≈ u₂

Under matched coupling, energy exchange is less likely to become a persistent drain. Growth remains bounded by individual capacities, not by a permanent leak.

This document does not derive a general maximum-power-transfer theorem for the coupled system; the formal result established here is the persistent-flow tax under mismatch (Section VII).

Debt D1 (partially closed by Theorem VI.2): The matching criterion above is a practical heuristic. Theorem VI.2 proves the network containment result — the persistent set is net-positive.

The rigorous quantitative criterion specifying the precise conditions on (a₁, a₂, u₁, u₂, k) under which a link is net-positive remains the owed within-class residual.

Impedance Mismatch

When a₁ ≠ a₂ or u₁ ≠ u₂, energy reflects or dissipates. Oscillation becomes friction. Interaction converts structure into heat.

The Default Boundary

Determining drift and capacity is noisy. Signals are delayed. Misclassification is expensive. The Operator’s default control law is:

k = 0 until match is verified.

Zero coupling prevents uncontrolled energy leakage into an entropy sink.

Structural Instability of Neutral Coupling

Consider the coupled dynamics under the assumption that each agent compensates its own drift exactly (u_i = a_i E_i). The simplified dynamics become:

Ė₁ = −k(E₁ − E₂)

Ė₂ = +k(E₁ − E₂)

Define the difference Δ = E₁ − E₂. Then Δ̇ = −2kΔ, which is stable: any nonzero Δ decays exponentially to zero. Under perfect drift compensation, coupling drives the system toward energy equality.

However, perfect compensation (u_i = a_i E_i) is not the cost-optimal strategy when effort is priced (λ > 0).

The cost-optimal steady state (Theorem II.1) satisfies u_i = a_i E_i where E_i = 1/(1 + λa_i²). If a₁ ≠ a₂, the optimal operating points differ: E₁ ≠ E₂.

The coupling term k(E₁ − E₂) is therefore nonzero at the cost-optimal equilibrium, representing a persistent energy flow from the agent with lower drift to the agent with higher drift.

The equal-energy condition is not the cost-optimal operating point when drift rates differ.

Any attempt to maintain E₁ = E₂ with a₁ ≠ a₂ requires the lower-drift agent to subsidise the higher-drift agent permanently—a tax on the more efficient system with no structural return.

Result

Theorem VI.1 (Coupling). Coupling creates a gradient channel. If one side cannot pay its own drift, the link becomes a permanent tax on the other.

When drift rates differ, the cost-optimal equilibrium has unequal energy levels, and the coupling term drives persistent flow from lower-drift to higher-drift agent. Zero coupling is the only safe default until match is verified.

You know this in your body. The relationship where you give and the other person drains. The partnership where one side cannot pay their own drift. The coupling converts your control authority into heat.

The mathematics says what your instinct already knows: decouple until match is verified.

The general result the Link defers — net-positive coupling across an arbitrary constraint network — is established here conditionally, reading the same dynamics Ėᵢ = −aᵢEᵢ + uᵢ + Σⱼ kᵢⱼ(Eⱼ − Eᵢ) at network scale: the drift drain aᵢEᵢ is the depletion each operator must cover, and a link is net-positive when the structured energy it delivers exceeds that drain after the channel’s irreducible overhead ε.

Theorem VI.2 (Net-Positive Containment). Consider a finite network of operators, each obeying Ėᵢ = −aᵢEᵢ + uᵢ + Σⱼ kᵢⱼ(Eⱼ − Eᵢ), each carrying a drift drain aᵢEᵢ it must cover from its own control budget, each link paying an irreducible overhead ε > 0. Suppose each operator can (i) read and act on its own viability gradient (detect Ėᵢ < 0 and respond), (ii) condition each link on the accumulated record of its counterparty, and (iii) set kᵢⱼ = 0 (sever / exit). Then as t → ∞, every persistent configuration — every configuration in which no operator’s Eᵢ reaches the no-return surface — is net-positive: each sustained link delivers structured energy exceeding the drift drain it must cover. Net-negative configurations (the mismatch sink of Theorem VI.1 driven past the taxed agent’s maintenance margin; free-riding; undefended parasitism; pure defection) each reach the no-return surface in finite time, on the order of budget ÷ (drain − surplus) steps. The balance point (surplus = drain) is the non-sustained boundary of the net-positive class, settled by almost-sure recurrence of the zero-mean budget walk, not finite expected time.

Theorem VI.1 and Theorem VI.2 are complementary, not redundant. VI.1 is the standing tax that drift mismatch imposes between two honest agents, each paying its own drift; VI.2 is the exclusion of extraction configurations — drain, free-riding, parasitism, defection — from the persistent set at network scale. VI.1’s mismatch tax is one way a link runs net-negative, feeding VI.2’s collapse condition; VI.2 generalises from the mismatch tax to any configuration whose net production falls below its drain.

Corollary (open networks). In any network open to new entrants, persistence further requires each operator to condition its links on the counterparty’s record — record-conditioned reciprocity — since a network of unconditional cooperators is invadable by a defector taking coupling benefit without paying its share. Budget class (net-positive) and strategy class (record-conditioned) are distinct: containment holds in the budget class; record-conditioning is forced only under entry.
