How to read the σ column▸

How to read the σ column. Every measurement comes with a bar: one standard deviation, σ, the experimenters’ own statement of how far their number might sit from the truth. The σ column divides the gap between prediction and measurement by that bar. The sign says which side: + above the measurement, − below. The Error column gives the same gap in the number’s own units. The odds below are the chance of landing at least that far from the truth by chance alone, counting both directions.

  • Under 1σ — inside the bar. Agreement: about two honest measurements in three land this close, and one in three lands further out by chance alone.
  • 2σ — tension. One in twenty-two. Worth watching, not yet a verdict.
  • 3σ — evidence of a real gap. One in 370.
  • 5σ — the discovery standard of particle physics. One in 1.7 million (one in 3.5 million if only one direction counts, as the discovery convention usually reads it). Each switch names its kill threshold in advance. Past it, the row is dead by the work’s own rule.
  • Above 5σ — dead. The two corpses on this page sit at 7.24σ and 5.7σ. They are shown, never repaired.

Einstein’s field equationsRecord and Constraint▸

From Record and Constraint. What has landed holds, and it holds somewhere. So the landscape — every grain that has already landed — bends where the next grain can fall. That bending is gravity, and Einstein’s equations are its mathematics: the equations behind GPS and black holes. A clock on a GPS satellite ticks faster than the one on your wrist.

Physics has tested the equations for a century. They pass. And it has a theorem for their shape. In 1971 and 1972 the mathematician David Lovelock proved that in three directions of space and one of time, only one equation of this kind keeps a short list of rules. Einstein’s, with room for one spare constant. The theorem is pure mathematics. The rules and the four dimensions go in. It does not say where they come from.

Here they are derived. The sheet and the grains are the same record. Space and time are not a stage the grains fall onto. They are the grains that have landed. Count them at each place and you have what physics calls the density of matter. One quantity, read two ways. Then the rules. Constraint gives one: a record is somewhere, and reaching anywhere else takes time, so each place answers only to its neighbours. Record is read as another: what has landed is neither unwritten nor made from nothing in a place, so it changes there only by flowing in or out. Earlier papers give the rest, and AP10 the count, three and one, on its own live condition (KS-D.3). Feed them to Lovelock’s theorem and one equation is left. Einstein’s, with room for the cosmological constant, Λ. Where the bending is gentle, things move slowly and Λ is too small to notice, it is Newton’s.

The Born ruleone record, many windows▸

From one record, read at many windows. In quantum mechanics you do not get the odds of an outcome directly. You get a number that points, like the hand of a clock. The odds are its length, squared. Not the length. Not the cube. The square. That is the Born rule, and every quantum experiment is read through it.

Max Born wrote it down in 1926. It has never failed. Most textbooks still give it as a postulate — a rule to start from, not a result. But physics has reasons. In 1957 Andrew Gleason proved that wherever a question has three or more answers that exclude each other — if an outcome’s odds do not depend on what else is asked beside it, and the odds of exclusive outcomes add up — the square is the only rule left. Later routes, from decision theory and symmetry, each start from something assumed. AP25 builds on Gleason.

Here it is derived. Gleason’s conditions come from the conditions of a record. A record is definite — this, not that. A mixed state, only a list of odds over other states, splits into such lists in endless ways and says no one thing. So a record is written from one pure state. That is Record. One crossing writes one record, and it holds two things: the state and the outcome. Every window reads through its own set of questions. The set is the window’s, not the record’s. It is not an event. So the odds cannot depend on it. That is Break and Record. And one crossing gives one outcome, so the odds of this or that are the two added. That is Symmetry, Break and Record together. Gleason’s theorem does the rest. The strangest rule in physics is what agreement between windows looks like.

Derived, with one outside input, and it is mathematics: Gleason’s theorem. With only two answers, nothing locks the odds, and other rules fit. That is not the three of space. A particle can be in endless places, so its full space has endless dimensions. A two-answer spin inherits the rule from it. The quantum space comes from AP09, and the step that builds it is still live: KS-Q.7. “The record holds two things” leans on the four conditions being complete. That proof is still owed. Its switch says so: KS-P.1.

It dies if an outcome’s odds change with what is asked beside it, beyond what quantum mechanics predicts: KS-55. It dies if any system breaks the square: KS-56. It dies if a record is written from a mixed state: KS-57. The contextuality already measured is something else — answers held before the asking, which Record denies anyway. All three are live.

Standard Model gauge group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1)the Break▸

From the Break. Electromagnetism runs electricity, magnets and light. The strong force holds the middle of an atom together. The weak force lets a star burn. Physics measured how each one works and never explained why.

It has the rule behind all three. A gauge group is a freedom to relabel something without changing anything you can measure. Where the label can change from place to place, something must keep neighbours in step. That something is a force (Weyl, 1929; Yang and Mills, 1954). U(1) turns one dial, and SU(2) turns a pair. Together they give the weak force and light, which part when the symmetry breaks. SU(3) turns three, called colours: the strong force. The Standard Model writes SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1) and takes it as given. It never says why these three.

Here each comes from the break. Every quantum state carries a phase, an angle the odds never see. Before any grain lands, the phase is one. The landscape gives it places, and it must be read from each. The field that reads it, once the weak force has broken away, is electromagnetism. Light is its ripple (AP15). The break sits between the two sides of the symmetry, so it carries two parts: how it stands to this side, and to that. Swap the labels, or blend them, and nothing you can measure changes. That freedom is U(2): locally, SU(2) and a U(1), hypercharge (AP27). Constraint, Symmetry and Break are the three faces behind space’s three directions. Each has a fixed job and no fixed place. None comes from another, so nothing says which face lines up with the break. That choice is colour. Not a turn in the room: an inner label, a quantum blend of the three faces. The turns of such blends are U(3). Take away the overall phase, which nothing measures, and SU(3) is left, not the rotations of space. Its turns do not commute, so gluons carry colour themselves (AP19).

Chiral coupling — unexplained since 1956, argued at the structural registerthe Break▸

From the Break. The weak force works one way round and not the other. Your hands are mirror images, and a left glove will not fit the right. Physics long believed nature could not tell them apart: whatever happens, its mirror image could happen too. That mirror rule is called parity. Light keeps it. The strong force keeps it. The weak force does not.

In 1956 Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang saw that nobody had ever tested it in the weak force. Chien-Shiung Wu and her team ran the test over the turn of 1956 into 1957. They chilled cobalt-60 almost to absolute zero, lined up its nuclei and counted the electrons thrown off. More went one way than the other. In a mirror, the count reverses. Nature can tell left from right. The part of the weak force that turns one particle into another touches only left-handed particles and right-handed antiparticles. For a particle near light speed, left-handed means spinning one way about its direction of travel. Every neutrino ever caught is left-handed. The Standard Model writes this in by hand. It has never said why.

Here it is because a break has a direction. The break runs one way, from the symmetry to the record. A landed grain does not unland. That is the arrow of time. AP22 finds the mirror between the two sides of the symmetry acting as CPT — matter swapped for antimatter, left for right, forward for backward — at a black hole’s horizon, and AP27 carries that reading to the weak force. In one hand, AP27 argues, the arrow runs against the particle’s twist, and the break’s two parts, the pair behind the weak force’s SU(2), stay free to be turned. In the other hand the arrow runs with the twist and locks the two parts into one. There is nothing to turn.

AP27 grades it structural, not derived: drawn from the conditions, consistent with every measurement since Wu, not yet a theorem. The formal coupling theorem is AP27’s principal open problem, and its switch says so: KS-63. It faces a known objection. CPT holds exactly in every tested theory, and an arrow read through it would first single out CP, which the weak force nearly keeps, not P, which it breaks outright. Meeting that is part of the open theorem. If the theorem never comes, the group SU(2) × U(1) still stands, and one-handedness goes back to being read off experiment.

Bell’s test, and the quantum ceiling S = 2√2the Break, at the now▸

From the Break, at the now. Two particles made together, in one state, sent to opposite ends of the world. Measure one, and the other answers it. Along the same direction, always the opposite: one up, one down, like the two halves of a broken plate. At an angle, only partly — by an amount the angle sets. Einstein called it spooky. Here it is not.

In 1964 John Bell found a test. If each particle carried its answers from the start, and nothing passed between them, a score called S could never pass 2. That is Bell’s line, in the form experiments use: the CHSH inequality of 1969. Quantum mechanics goes past it, to 2√2, about 2.83, and no further. That ceiling is not Bell’s. Boris Tsirelson proved it in 1980. Stuart Freedman and John Clauser saw the line crossed in 1972. Alain Aspect crossed it in 1982, changing the settings while the particles flew. The tests of 2015 closed the two main loopholes at once. None has gone above 2√2. The 2022 Nobel Prize in physics went to this work.

Before the fall there is no distance: the two are one wave. Their paths are written in the landscape, far apart. The wave is not written yet, and what is not written has no here and there. The fall writes one record with two ends. One break. Both answers. Nothing travels between them, because there is no between. No message can ride on it: what one end sees alone never changes with what is done at the other. And Record allows no answers waiting before the fall. So Bell’s line does not bind. How far past it the pair goes is a calculation: the Born rule on the shared state, at the best four angles, gives 2√2 exactly.

Derived, on the paper’s grade. The 2√2 stands on the Born rule, derived in AP25 with Gleason’s theorem, and on AP09’s quantum space, whose building step is still live: KS-Q.7. So “from structure” means the standard quantum answer, reached from the work’s own premises. The paper shows the pair reaches 2√2. That nothing goes higher is Tsirelson’s theorem — named, not proved again. Answers carried from the start are ruled out twice. The local kind, by experiment. The kind linked across the distance, by Record, as a premise — no experiment can separate it, because a theory like Bohm’s gives the same numbers. That switch, KS-49a, is closed on those terms.

It dies if the agreement fades with distance, or changes with which end is measured first, beyond what quantum mechanics predicts: KS-48a. It dies if a message is ever sent through the pair: KS-48b. It dies if measuring one end leaves the whole only partly written: KS-54. All three are live.

Special relativity’s kinematics, and the sign of the signatureConstraint and Record, through the windows▸

From Constraint and Record, read through the windows. Nothing can go faster than light, and the faster a clock travels, the slower it ticks. Cosmic rays make particles high in the air that should mostly die before reaching the ground. They arrive. In our reading, their clocks run slow.

Einstein built special relativity in 1905 on two rules taken as given. From 1910, Ignatowsky and others showed plainer starting points do most of the work: no observer special, space and time alike everywhere, space alike in every direction, views that combine consistently. They leave three possible worlds. Galileo’s, with no top speed. One where time turns into space, like a map being turned. And Einstein’s. Measurement picks Einstein’s. The turning world is ruled out by one more demand: a cause comes before its effect.

That is the sign of the signature. On a map, distance is Pythagoras: square the steps across and up, add, and take the root. In spacetime the time part is subtracted. That minus is the sign. With a plus, time would be one more direction of space, and a turn could carry later round to earlier. With the minus, two events never swap when one depends on the other.

Here it comes from the windows. Every window keeps its own count of ticks. There is no master clock. Two windows drifting apart each read the other’s as the slower. Neither is wrong. Distance is what light writes between windows, so the speed of light is the ruler, not a speed limit somebody found. The standard argument, cited as others’, then runs. Record makes the last demand: what is written cannot be unwritten, so no reading may put an event before a record it depends on. That is the minus.

And “now” is the grain still falling. No window can read its own, so there is no reading of it to share. The now they read from is one. Every view is relative. None is the right one. The now is the paper’s own step. It is derived. So is the sign. Two premises are ruled, not derived: that distance is the light-crossing, and that its echo returns the same in every direction at every window. Another, that views combine consistently, is stated. One debt is open: that the record’s own algebra makes the crossing its distance. The equivalence principle and the landscape’s curvaturethe neck▸

From the neck. Gravity is the neck. Cut the cable of a lift and you float. Inside, falling and floating in empty space feel the same. Stand on the floor and it pushes. Inside, a pile pulling you down and a rocket pushing you up feel the same. Those are the two halves of what Einstein called the equivalence principle. And everything falls alike: on the Moon in 1971, a hammer and a feather, dropped together, landed together. Einstein built general relativity on it as a postulate: assumed, then tested — now to about one part in a thousand trillion.

The neck is the waist of the hourglass, where a grain passes from what could happen to what has happened. Gravity depends on all twenty-one of its gates at once. It is not a force that moves things. It keeps them in place. Gravity keeps the whole pile from sliding. Weight is that keeping, felt against the resistance you are made of.

In sand the two halves are one sentence: a window reads its own holding, and nothing past it. The falling lift is not kept in place, so it reads no keeping in place. The lift on the floor reads only the pull against its own resistance. A window reads the landscape only by crossings. So the principle stops where a window is big enough to lie across two slopes. Then it reads the difference. Put a pile of sand between two places. Light passing the pile bends, and its crossing takes longer. Both are measured: starlight bent by the Sun, radar echoes delayed passing it. Here the longer crossing is a longer distance. Distance changed, and nothing moved. Far away, you call the change of slope curvature. Up close it is where the grain can go.

The equivalence principle is derived at the structural register: from what a window can and cannot read, in the picture’s own terms. What would make it exact waits on AP08’s field equation. Where the principle stops is ruled. That hammer and feather fall alike is not derived. It rests on a ruling: every window is the same sand. The bending of light is ruled, and so is the change of slope as curvature; the longer crossing follows. Which road a grain takes, and why a deep clock runs slow, are named, not derived. The full form of the pull of what could land is still owed; AP43 has its Newtonian form (AP43-D1).
